## HK$16 Billion Is Not a Single Lump Sum

Any financing announcement requires reading at least five data points: instrument type, size, price per share or interest rate, net proceeds, and use of funds—including timing. Media often reduce this to “HK$16 billion raised,” but this round combines a share placement and convertible bonds. Ordinary shares cause immediate dilution; bonds carry cash-cost obligations today and conversion risk later.

Founder zero compensation also doesn’t mean zero corporate cost. Executives may still hold shares, receive dividends, or assume other roles. The company still pays its R&D teams, compute costs, and marketing expenses. It’s a signal of commitment and alignment—not standalone proof of improved governance or an operational inflection point.

The 4% team incentive and 1% open-source allocation are reported using total issued share capital as the denominator. But key questions remain unverified: Where do these shares come from? Who receives them? Over what timeline? How are they accounted for? And how will the open-source fund be governed? The text preserves this two-tier distinction—stating the headline percentages while refraining from presenting execution plans as already finalized.

## Which Headlines Misrepresent Transaction Status

- **Execution Failure**: Citing only the 4% and 1% commitments—but presenting undisclosed vesting schedules and fund governance as already implemented.  
- **Status Failure**: Describing proposed financing, subscription agreements, or pre-closing arrangements as if cash has already landed.  
- **Compensation Failure**: Expanding “no salary” into “full forfeiture of shares, dividends, and all economic benefits.”  
- **Causal Failure**: Asserting that funding *guarantees* model leadership—without citing any operational metrics.  
- **Source Failure**: Relying solely on screenshots from social media—without official company or exchange documentation.

The two most common misreadings are:  
(1) Treating the pledged 4% and 1% as *already transferred shares*, rather than conditional future grants;  
(2) Presenting share placements, convertible bond subscriptions, and potential future conversions as if cash is already in the bank—or equity is already diluted.  
The first ignores contractual conditions for execution; the second collapses legally distinct actions across different dates into a single moment—both exaggerate actual progress.

The open letter also refines the share commitment language versus earlier drafts: Yan Junjie pledged to allocate, over the next four years, shares equivalent to 4% of the company’s *total issued share capital* for team incentives—and to dedicate 1% of total issued share capital to an open-source community fund. Crucially, both percentages refer to *“equivalent to total issued share capital”*—not 4% or 1% of his personal holdings. The final legal instruments, beneficiaries, vesting conditions, and fund governance still require formal documentation.

## Four Dates Must Be Tracked Separately

The placement agreement, convertible bond issuance, actual settlement, and future conversion are *not* the same event—and do not occur on the same date. The figure `HKD 9.54 billion + HKD 6.5 billion = HKD 16.04 billion` reflects only the nominal size announced; net proceeds must deduct underwriting and other fees. Ordinary shares change immediately upon completion of the placement—but convertible bonds enter the share capital *only* after conversion conditions are met *and* conversion is executed. The 4% team incentive and 1% open-source fund allocations also require further documentation to confirm funding sources and governance. Mapping all these events onto a single timeline prevents double-counting dilution.

Analysts should retain original currencies—HKD and USD—and avoid forced FX conversions without specified exchange-rate dates. Revenue, losses, and cash balances are reported in USD; placements and bonds are denominated in HKD. When comparing across currencies, explicitly state both the exchange rate used *and* its effective date—so FX fluctuations aren’t mistaken for operational shifts. The first post-funding financial report warrants close scrutiny of three items: cash balance, R&D spending, and financing-related expenses. Together, they determine how much the new capital actually extends the company’s runway. If only gross funding amounts are available—without net proceeds or disbursement progress—mark the item as “pending verification.” Gross funding totals are not usable runway.
