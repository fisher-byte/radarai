## From Sunway 8000 to 70-Second Pharmacy Dispensing: What’s Already Live?

- **Models**: Verify official model ID, open-weight status, license terms, pricing, and benchmark configurations.  
- **Agents**: Confirm connected systems, executable actions, permission scope, audit logging, and human-in-the-loop fallback.  
- **Robots**: Document control mode (autonomous vs. remote), continuous runtime, payload capacity, failure modes, and maintenance requirements.  
- **Compute**: Record chip architecture, cluster scale, supported frameworks, throughput, and power efficiency.  
- **Industrial AI**: Track production line, dataset size, baseline metrics, measurable improvement, and operational duration.

First-time announcements reflect event scale—not product readiness. A “global premiere” may still be in closed beta. Meanwhile, an unannounced industrial deployment might have run smoothly for months. Prioritize procurement based on evidence strength:  
✅ Publicly accessible product + documentation  
✅ Reproducible demo under real conditions  
✅ Named customer + documented baseline  
✅ Signed contract / active mass production  
—each step increases weight.

Governance forums aren’t window dressing. Cross-border data flows, model safety, content watermarking, and sector-specific accountability will directly constrain deployment. Engineering teams should log at least one capability *and* one compliance constraint per relevant product—so post-event follow-up goes beyond a simple feature checklist.

## Buyer Team Case Study: How to Conduct a Booth-Level Evidence Audit

Have enterprise innovation teams process 30 candidates within 14 days post-conference:  
6 models, 8 agents, 6 robots, 6 industrial AI solutions, and 4 compute/governance offerings.  
Each candidate must be evaluated using *only one evidence card*—no sales decks allowed.

| Stage | Booth Verification Action | Pass Criteria |
|---|---|---|
| **Collection** | Official page, product documentation, on-site materials, and one external source | All 30 candidates have timestamped evidence |
| **Classification** | Categorized as `live` / `beta` / `demo` / `announced` | Status matches linked evidence |
| **Task Design** | Write a test that can run in ≤2 hours per candidate | Clear input, action, and acceptance criteria |
| **Screening** | Score each candidate on business relevance, evidence quality, cost, and integration effort | Only top 5 advance to pilot phase |
| **Pause** | No documentation, no named contact, or only video clips provided | Move to “watch list”—excluded from procurement shortlist |

Buyer teams do *not* rank candidates by booth buzz. Instead, for each candidate they preserve four evidence types:  
- Official webpage  
- Product documentation  
- On-site input/output samples  
- External validation  

Different verification checklists apply to: live systems, deployed products, preview software, and roadmap items. If a vendor cannot provide a point-of-contact, documentation, or clear deployment boundaries, the solution goes into the *lead pool*—not the procurement shortlist.

## Four Most Common Misjudgments at Conferences

- **List Failure**: Listing dozens of products without indicating status, access method, or user task.  
- **Launch Failure**: Assuming “conference debut” equals production-ready or mass-deployable.  
- **Case Failure**: Citing industrial performance gains (e.g., “+30% efficiency”) without baseline, timeframe, or sample size.  
- **Procurement Failure**: Adding vendors to the shortlist solely due to crowded booths or media coverage.  
- **Update Failure**: Failing to track post-event changes in documentation, pricing, pilot progress, or delivery timelines.

“First appearance” does not mean purchasable.  
“Live demo” ≠ unattended operation.  
“Company claims 20–100× speedup” ≠ reproducible on your query set.  
“A 3-year roadmap” ≠ full feature delivery by 2026.  
Any inventory lacking maturity labeling is just a hotword dump—not an actionable assessment.

## At the Same Conference, Model Launches, Live Demos, and Industry Projects Belong in Three Separate Ledgers

The WAIC official site scatters forum and exhibition info across disjoint pages. The legacy `/activity` path returns 404; the [forum page](https://www.worldaic.com.cn/events/forum) remains accessible for verifying session names, timing, and organizers. Editors should first categorize every item into three distinct tables:  
- Model/product launches  
- Live demos  
- Industry projects or signed agreements  

Evidence requirements differ sharply across categories—so they must *never* be ranked together on a single “major announcement” leaderboard.

A model launch requires at minimum: model ID, access URL, model card, pricing, and license terms.  
For example: Qwen3.8-Max was still in *Preview* during WAIC; Kimi K3 already had a live API and pricing page—but its full weights are scheduled for release before July 27.  
Labeling both as “trillion-parameter models” obscures critical delivery-stage differences.  
The WAIC overview page should retain explicit stage tags—and route parameter specs, cost details, and testing guidance into separate, dedicated articles.

Live demonstrations must document the control chain and reproduction conditions. In robot combat using real-time motion capture, what viewers see is a low-latency mapping system—combining full-body control and hardware execution—not fully autonomous decision-making. The same applies to agent demos: if someone pre-logs into the system, prepares data, and scopes the task beforehand, success in the demo cannot directly imply readiness for enterprise environments regarding permissions, exception recovery, or concurrency handling. Such demos still hold value—but deliverables should be the precise inputs and steps needed for the next reproducible test.

For industrial projects or signed contracts, track the responsible party, contract value, construction timeline, acceptance milestones, and post-deployment operations. The total project value announced in closing press releases reflects only event scale—not procurement relevance. What matters more to buyers is being able to locate, 90 days later, the point person, product documentation, pilot customers, and baseline performance metrics. This article’s “30 Candidate Tasks” requirement—that each item fits on a single evidence card—is precisely intended to compress event-day hype into traceable, actionable fields.

A fourth ledger—dedicated to governance—is equally essential. Model safety, cross-border data flows, content watermarking, and industry-specific accountability rarely appear in demo scoring—but can block deployment entirely. Every candidate entering pilot testing must log *both* one capability proof *and* one data/compliance constraint. Products with capability evidence but no documented data lineage should not make the procurement shortlist.

All three are trending—but their next steps differ sharply: repository regression testing, long-document cost benchmarking, and perturbation resilience testing. This approach surfaces *actionable divergence*, not just popularity rankings.
